Why this, why me

Three things I would bring to a studio building from zero

01

Moving a loved thing somewhere new

iPlayer was the BBC putting its relationship with the audience onto a platform that did not exist yet. The Fuelband put a brand built on physical performance into a connected product. Bedrock brought 120+ Diageo brands onto one system without flattening what made each of them distinct. That is the risk this venture is carrying: go somewhere genuinely new, and do not damage what people already love on the way.

02

Design your engineers can actually build

Bedrock pushed design tokens into production UI rather than into a handover deck. We took an off-the-shelf component baseline to get launch velocity, then refactored into brand-flexible tokens once the system could carry it. I work in the repo as well as the file, and I am not going to hand a team a specification that leaves the hard parts to someone else.

03

Children online, and the duty that comes with it

iPlayer carried a public-service duty of care to a household audience. At HSBC I set the trust and control boundaries for generative AI inside a regulated bank. At Ford I owned an information hierarchy validated as safety-critical. Different domains, one discipline: build the guardrails into the product instead of auditing them afterwards. That matters more, not less, when the players are children.

How I think about play

Digital and physical play run on the same mechanics

Digital play and physical play are founded on the same principle. The interface may be different, but the mechanics are exactly the same.

A child stacking bricks and a child inside a game loop are doing the same thing: setting a goal, testing it, adjusting, going again. That is why I do not think of a move into games as a change of discipline. The loop is the loop. What changes is the fidelity of the feedback, how fast it comes back, and how many other people are inside it with you.

It is also why I am confident about the transfer. The Fuelband turned effort into something you could see and chase, and it was judged on engagement rather than conversion. Flavour Print read reactions in real time and gave something personal back. At Diageo I ran continuous experimentation on live products, where one test moved conversion 10% and about $9M. None of that was a game. All of it was designing a loop, measuring whether people stayed in it, and changing it while it was running.

The thing I would argue hardest about is what the loop is for. There is a version of this craft that optimises for time spent, and it works, and it is the wrong instinct for a company whose brand is built on what children make rather than how long you held their attention.

The honest part

I have never shipped a game

Not one. No engine experience, no live-ops rotation, no shipped title with my name in the credits, and I am saying it to a studio whose president has thirty years of doing exactly that. Dressing up adjacent work as though it were the same thing would waste everybody's afternoon.

What I would actually be useful for is the part that is not game craft. Standing up a design practice inside a new studio. Making the system hold when the team triples. Getting design and engineering to agree on where the tokens live before it becomes an argument. Carrying a brand into somewhere new without the people who love it feeling that something has been taken from them. Those are the problems I have been paid to solve for twenty five years, and they are the ones a venture at this stage tends to hit before it hits the interesting ones.

The game design itself I would expect to learn from people who already know it, and to defer to them for a long time before I had an opinion worth listening to.
